Airbnb Satisfaction & Price Prediction

A rebuilt two-part modelling pipeline that classifies unusually high guest ratings and predicts nightly prices without leaking information from validation or test data.

Population9,954 listings at or below $1,000
Evaluation60 / 20 / 20 split
ToolsR · LASSO · Random forest · XGBoost
View cleaned R pipeline

01

Two business questions

Guest satisfaction

Can listing and host information distinguish properties whose average across seven review measures exceeds 4.9 out of 5?

Nightly price

How accurately can the same listing information predict advertised nightly price for a previously unseen property?

Decision scope

The analysis supports targeting and pricing discussions. It does not prove that changing a feature causes ratings to rise, and price prediction is not the same as forecasting revenue or investment return.

02

Rebuilt evaluation pipeline

I retained the original modelling ambition but separated model development from final evaluation. Every transformation is learned from the training sample, candidate models are selected on validation data, and the test sample is opened once for the final report.

  1. 1
    Define the population

    Limit the analysis to 9,954 Melbourne listings with advertised prices no higher than $1,000.

  2. 2
    Create the outcomes

    Build the High Average Score flag from the seven review measures and keep raw price for final dollar-scale evaluation.

  3. 3
    Split before preprocessing

    Create training, validation and test samples before imputing values, grouping rare categories or creating dummy variables.

  4. 4
    Compare three model families

    Use sparse LASSO models as interpretable baselines, random forests for bagged nonlinear structure and XGBoost for sequential error correction.

  5. 5
    Report untouched test results

    Choose the model and classification threshold with validation data, then assess the frozen choices on unseen listings.

05

What drove the predictions

Room type, bathrooms, bedrooms, guest capacity and location were the leading price predictors by XGBoost gain. Host and review history also contributed, particularly host tenure, review recency and availability.

Useful signals
  • Room type carried the strongest price signal.
  • Bathrooms, bedrooms and capacity captured listing size.
  • Latitude and longitude allowed local price patterns without forcing broad suburb averages.
  • Recent review and availability history added behavioural context.
Interpretation limits
  • Feature importance measures predictive contribution, not causation.
  • Several variables may reflect an operating listing rather than a property available before launch.
  • High-priced properties remain harder to predict and drive RMSE upward.
  • A single random split should eventually be replaced with repeated or time-based evaluation.

06

What I corrected

Preprocessing leakage

The earlier notebook imputed numerical values before splitting the data. The new recipe learns medians, category handling and dummy columns from training rows only.

Inconsistent category handling

Rare levels were previously grouped independently in training and test sets. The rebuilt recipe learns the grouping once and maps unseen levels safely.

Validation reused as a test

The earlier XGBoost model used the same holdout for early stopping and the reported final score. The revised design keeps a third, untouched test sample.

Default classification cutoff

A 0.50 threshold produced high specificity but poor sensitivity. The new operating point is chosen on validation data and reported with both error types.

Back-transformation bias

Simply exponentiating log-price predictions tends to understate dollar prices. The rebuilt regression applies a training-based smearing correction.

Causal overreach

The earlier narrative turned model associations into action claims. The portfolio version separates predictive evidence from claims about what would improve a listing.

07

How I would explain it in an interview

60-second explanation

“I built two models from roughly 10,000 Melbourne Airbnb listings: one to identify unusually high guest ratings and another to predict nightly price. When I revisited the assignment, I found that some preprocessing happened before the split and the XGBoost validation set was also being used as the final evaluation. I rebuilt the workflow with separate training, validation and test samples. XGBoost achieved a test AUC of 0.760 for high ratings and a price RMSE of $95.6, compared with $167 for a simple median baseline. More importantly, I can explain why the evaluation is credible, how the threshold changes sensitivity and specificity, and why feature importance is useful for prediction but not proof of causation.”

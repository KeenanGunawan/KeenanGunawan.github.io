Bonus-Malus Scheme Design & Profit Analysis

A comparison of three experience-rating designs using Markov chains, Monte Carlo simulation, long-run profitability and Loimaranta efficiency.

Portfolio10,000 policies
ToolsR · Base statistics
Simulation2,000 scenarios
View cleaned R analysis

01

The decision question

An insurer was considering whether to retain its five-level bonus-malus system, simplify it to three levels, or introduce a more detailed seven-level design for younger drivers. I assessed which structure best balanced premium income, responsiveness to claim frequency, customer clarity and risk-based pricing.

My role

I built the transition matrices, simulated the next policy year, calculated stationary distributions and long-run profit, measured Loimaranta efficiency, and translated the trade-offs into a product recommendation.

02

Portfolio data and assumptions

The data contained one row per policyholder with age and current no-claim-discount level. I validated the population before modelling and kept the two risk groups separate throughout the corrected analysis.

Younger drivers5,000 policiesAges 20–25 · claim frequency 0.22
Older drivers5,000 policiesAges 50–55 · claim frequency 0.16
Claim severity$2,500 average costHeld constant across groups
Data checks

10,000 unique policy numbers · no missing required values · no ages outside the two modelled groups · all starting levels within −1 to 3

03

Approach

  1. 1
    Translate claim counts into transitions

    Used Poisson probabilities for zero, one, and two-or-more claims to populate each scheme’s transition matrix.

  2. 2
    Simulate the next policy year

    Moved each policyholder from their actual starting level and recalculated premium and claim cost across 2,000 scenarios.

  3. 3
    Find the long-run mix

    Solved for each transition matrix’s stationary distribution and converted the level mix into annual premium income.

  4. 4
    Measure pricing responsiveness

    Calculated Loimaranta efficiency across claim frequencies from 0.01 to 1.00.

  5. 5
    Audit the implementation

    Retested group assumptions and premium multipliers before comparing profit with risk differentiation.

04

2026 simulation under the current scheme

The current five-level design produced a stable premium base. Profit varied more than premium income because the number of simulated claims directly affected claim cost.

Mean premium$6.271m

Standard deviation of approximately $9.0k across 2,000 scenarios.

Mean profit$1.518m

After applying the simulated claim count and $2,500 average severity.

Profit variability$102.3k SD

The middle 90% of outcomes ran from about $1.349m to $1.691m.

Interpretation

Premium income was predictable because movements across 10,000 policies diversified. Claim-count variation remained the main source of one-year profit uncertainty.

06

Model review and corrections

I revisited the original analysis while preparing this portfolio. The audit found that the group-specific starting distributions, claim frequencies and stated premium multipliers needed to be applied more consistently. Correcting them changed the long-run ranking, especially for Option 2.

Starting levels

The simulation now uses the actual level distribution for each age group instead of scaling the combined portfolio distribution.

Age-specific frequency

Long-run calculations now use λ = 0.22 for younger drivers and λ = 0.16 for older drivers under every applicable scheme.

Premium multipliers

The seven-level young-driver design now matches the stated discounts: 40% discount through 80% penalty.

Reproducibility

The cleaned analysis validates the data, fixes the random seed and avoids a specialist Markov-chain dependency.

Lesson

A technically plausible output is not enough. Scheme definitions, state ordering and group assumptions must reconcile before a recommendation is made.

07

Recommendation and next iteration

Under the fixed assumptions, Option 1 maximises long-run profit but provides the weakest risk differentiation. Option 2 responds best to claim frequency but its stated discounts reduce profit substantially. I would not launch either alternative from these results alone.

Recommended next step

Recalibrate Option 2’s premium multipliers and test profit, retention and customer migration together. A targeted design could retain its strong risk responsiveness without giving away as much premium income.

60-second interview explanation

“I compared three motor-insurance bonus-malus schemes using Poisson claim counts, Markov-chain transition matrices, a 2,000-run Monte Carlo simulation and Loimaranta efficiency. The current scheme produced about $1.52 million expected profit for the next year. During a later audit, I corrected how age-group assumptions and premium multipliers fed the long-run model. That changed the conclusion: the simplified scheme had the highest profit, while the complex scheme had the best risk responsiveness. My recommendation was to recalibrate the complex tariffs and test retention before implementation. The project taught me how important model validation is when a small coding assumption can reverse a business decision.”

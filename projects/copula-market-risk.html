Copula Tail Risk & ARMA Forecasting

A ten-year study of how dependence assumptions change the estimated downside risk of an equally weighted NVDA–AMD portfolio—and why short-horizon mean forecasts add much less value.

Sample2,517 paired daily returns
Risk levels95% · 99% · 99.5%
MethodsVaR · ES · Copulas · ARMA
View reproducible R code

01

The risk question

Two assets can look diversified when assessed separately but still fall together in stressed markets. I tested how independence, Gaussian, Student-t and extreme dependence assumptions change portfolio VaR and Expected Shortfall while keeping the empirical NVDA and AMD return distributions fixed.

Portfolio50% NVDA · 50% AMDLoss is measured on one dollar invested
Main decisionChoose a dependence modelCompare estimates against empirical portfolio risk
Secondary testForecast five returnsAssess ARMA against a zero-return benchmark
Why this matters

Assuming independence reduced the estimated 99% portfolio VaR from the empirical 7.85% to 5.39%. The dependence model is therefore a material risk-management choice, not a cosmetic modelling detail.

02

Data and risk measures

I used adjusted closing prices from 1 June 2016 to 5 June 2026. Daily log-returns were converted to losses as L = 1 − exp(X), so larger positive values represent worse outcomes. VaR gives a loss threshold; ES reports the average loss at or beyond that threshold.

AMD has the higher standalone VaR and ES at every confidence level. These marginal measures do not reveal whether the two stocks experience extreme losses at the same time.

Model choice

The t-copula with ν = 10 best matches overall VaR and ES. The heavier-tailed ν = 3 model better matches conditional lower-tail frequency, so no single model dominates every objective.

05

Downside dependence is stronger than upside dependence

Maximum-likelihood selection among the permitted Archimedean families chose a Survival BB1 copula. Unlike a symmetric t-copula, it can represent stronger co-movement in negative extremes than in positive extremes.

Lower-tail dependence0.527

Severe negative returns have substantial joint-tail dependence.

Upper-tail dependence0.066

Extreme positive returns are much less tightly coupled.

99% model fit7.64% / 9.83%

VaR / ES versus empirical 7.85% / 9.82%.

Balanced interpretation

Survival BB1 is compelling when asymmetric downside dependence matters. It still overstates the simulated 99.5% conditional joint-tail probability, so maximum likelihood does not guarantee the best fit for every tail statistic.

06

ARMA models explain more than they forecast

Adjusted prices were non-stationary, while daily returns were approximately weakly stationary. AIC selected an MA(2) for NVDA and an ARMA(2,2) for AMD. Both models passed a lag-20 Ljung–Box check at 5%, but their five-day forecasts remained close to zero.

NVDAARIMA(0,0,2)2.050%2.111%80%0.087

Practical result

The models marginally beat a zero-return forecast but miss most realised magnitudes. Five observations are far too few to claim reliable predictive skill, and visible volatility clustering suggests ARMA–GARCH as a stronger next step.

07

What I improved for the portfolio version

Date alignment made explicit

The public script aligns both return series by trading date before any joint calculation, making the pairing auditable.

Portable output directory

The submitted R Markdown tried to save figures beside the source file and failed when that location was not writeable. The cleaned script creates a predictable output folder in the working directory.

Reusable functions

VaR, ES, empirical inverse transforms, tail probabilities and manual ARMA forecasts are separated into named functions.

Decision metrics separated

Overall VaR/ES accuracy, conditional joint-tail frequency and likelihood fit are reported as different model-selection objectives.

Forecast claim constrained

A tiny five-day test is presented as a diagnostic comparison rather than evidence of dependable market prediction.

Limitations retained

Copula parameters are time-invariant, extreme-tail samples are small, and ARMA models the conditional mean rather than changing volatility.

08

How I would explain it in an interview

60-second explanation

“I analysed ten years of NVDA and AMD returns to see how dependence assumptions affect an equally weighted portfolio’s tail risk. AMD was riskier on a standalone basis, but the main result was joint dependence: assuming independence reduced 99% VaR from the empirical 7.85% to 5.39%. Among the benchmark models, a t-copula with ten degrees of freedom best matched VaR and ES. A maximum-likelihood Survival BB1 model showed why asymmetry matters—estimated lower-tail dependence was 0.527 versus only 0.066 in the upper tail. I also fitted ARMA models, but their five-day forecasts only marginally beat zero and missed realised magnitudes. The project taught me that modelling joint downside behaviour was more valuable here than trying to predict the next daily return.”

Election Polling & Demographic Analysis

A statistical walkthrough of how I estimated voter support, compared demographic patterns and tested whether apparent differences were supported by the data.

Dataset1,453 likely voters
ToolsR · tidyverse
MethodsIntervals · z-tests
View cleaned R code

01

The question

The analysis used a simplified Georgia polling dataset to answer a decision-focused question: what does the sample suggest about candidate support, which demographic patterns are most visible, and how much confidence should we place in those findings?

My role

I completed the data summaries, statistical calculations, hypothesis tests and written interpretation in R. I then translated the findings into recommendations while acknowledging the poll’s limitations.

02

Data and tools

The sample contained voting preference alongside four demographic variables: gender, race, age group and income band. The work was completed in R using readxl, dplyr, tidyr and base statistical functions.

03

Approach

  1. 1
    Validate and summarise

    Checked the available fields and converted raw counts into overall and conditional proportions.

  2. 2
    Quantify uncertainty

    Estimated candidate support and constructed confidence intervals around each proportion.

  3. 3
    Compare segments

    Grouped observations by demographic characteristics to identify where support differed most.

  4. 4
    Test, then interpret

    Used two-proportion z-tests for gender comparisons and separated statistical evidence from practical interpretation.

  5. 5
    Stress-test the conclusion

    Reviewed sampling, response and timing biases before forming a recommendation.

Interpretation

The estimates indicate a sample lead, not a guaranteed election outcome. Representativeness, non-response and changes after the polling date can matter more than the reported sampling error.

05

Demographic patterns

I calculated support within each demographic group rather than comparing raw counts. This makes groups of different sizes easier to compare.

The gap narrowed most among respondents aged 65 and above. That is a descriptive pattern in this sample; it should not be treated as causal evidence about why an age group preferred a candidate.

See additional demographic observations
  • Gender percentages were relatively close for both leading candidates.
  • The largest race-level gaps appeared among Hispanic and Black respondents, although subgroup sample sizes were not supplied in the report and should be checked before drawing strong conclusions.
  • Candidate A’s support increased in the highest income category, while Candidate B’s support was strongest in the lower and middle bands.

06

Testing the gender difference

A visual difference does not automatically imply a population difference. I tested each leading candidate’s support using a two-sided two-proportion z-test at the 5% significance level.

# Structure of the comparison
p_pool <- (count_female + count_male) / (total_female + total_male)
z <- (p_female - p_male) /
  sqrt(p_pool * (1 - p_pool) * (1 / total_female + 1 / total_male))
p_value <- 2 * (1 - pnorm(abs(z)))
Candidate Ap = 0.582

No statistically significant gender difference detected.

Candidate Bp = 0.390

No statistically significant gender difference detected.

What this means

The test does not prove that support is identical across genders. It means the observed differences were not large enough, relative to sampling variability, to reject equality at the chosen threshold.

07

Limitations

Sample representation

A large sample does not eliminate bias if it does not reflect the voting population on relevant characteristics.

Response behaviour

Non-response and socially desirable answers may create systematic error that a confidence interval does not capture.

Timing

Polls measure a moment. News, campaigns and turnout intentions can change after collection.

Dependence assumptions

Candidate proportions from the same multinomial sample are negatively dependent. A future sample-size calculation should model that covariance explicitly rather than treating them as independent.

08

Reflection and next iteration

This project strengthened my ability to move from a statistical output to a defensible decision narrative. The most important lesson was that technical correctness includes explaining what the analysis cannot establish.

What worked
  • Clear conditional comparisons
  • Appropriate use of uncertainty
  • Connection between results and decisions
What I would improve
  • Automate validation checks
  • Report subgroup sample sizes
  • Model covariance in sample-size work
  • Add reproducible visualisations

60-second interview explanation

“I analysed a poll of 1,453 likely voters in R. I estimated overall candidate support with confidence intervals, compared preferences across gender, age, race and income, and used two-proportion tests to check whether the apparent gender differences were statistically meaningful. The main challenge was separating visible sample patterns from defensible population conclusions. If I repeated the project, I would strengthen the reproducibility checks and explicitly model dependence between candidate proportions in the sample-size analysis.”
